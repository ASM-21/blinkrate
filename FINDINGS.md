# Findings

Eight questions, eight answers, each with how it was identified and what would
overturn it. Every number is regenerated by `python -m blinkrate.run_all` and
checked against this text by `python tools/check_claims.py`, which exits
non-zero on drift.

**Standing.** The critique of IEEE 1366's 2.5-beta method is established prior
work: the threshold's instability was identified in the IEEE literature by 2013,
and LBNL both evaluated alternative major-event definitions and documented that
state commissions use incompatible ones. Q1 and Q2 are corroboration at national
scale. See [LITERATURE.md](LITERATURE.md) for what was searched and what it
changed.

**Scope conditions, shared by every answer below.** US investor-owned,
cooperative and municipal distribution utilities filing EIA-861; 2013-2018 and
2022; annual aggregates only, never daily series; no spending variable anywhere;
no external weather index. Q1 and Q8's counterfactuals are model-implied under
IEEE 1366's own log-normality assumption and are not measurements.

**Falsification criterion for the repository as a whole.** The central claim is
that measurement rules move reported distribution reliability more than the
measured differences between utilities do. It fails if the threshold sweep spans
materially less than the observed cross-utility spread in reported SAIDI, or if
the rank reversal in Q3 disappears once utilities are compared within like storm
exposure.

**The criterion has been run, and part one fails. The central claim, as stated,
is falsified** (attacks A8, A10 to A13).

> **Withdrawn.** Versions 1.0 and 1.1 reported that "the criterion has been run,
> and both halves hold", and that moving the threshold from 1.5 to 3.0 changes one
> large utility's reported SAIDI by a median of 6.18x against a 3.92x spread
> across the industry, so that the rule moved one utility's number by 1.58 times
> the whole p10-to-p90 spread across the industry. That comparison is true only
> inside IEEE 1366's own log-normal model on a window the evidence does not
> support, and it should not be quoted as a finding about the grid.

*Part one* compared one utility's own threshold sweep with the spread between
utilities. The sweep is model-implied, and version 1.1 had already shown the
comparison rests on two analyst choices: it is conditional on the tails of daily
outages (attack A10) and on how wide a threshold window counts as a real
alternative (A11). Under log-normal tails A11 needed a window wide enough that
the standard's model would declare 14.7 major event days a year at its loose end. The second literature pass (LITERATURE.md) says where both
choices actually sit.

**The window.** IEEE's working group designed 2.5 as a frequency target, about
2.27 qualifying days a year, not as a multiplier with a natural range. So the
defensible alternatives are other frequencies, and the evidence brackets them:
one excluded day a year (Italy's regulatory guarantee), two to eight (what the
working group observed on donated utility data), and fifteen (one Canadian
utility's whole-province count in a bad year). Converted to thresholds through
the standard's own model, the widest of these runs from beta
1.74 to 2.78. On every
evidence-grounded window, under every tail model this repository uses, one
utility's sweep is **smaller** than the spread between utilities. The best ratio
anywhere is **0.86**, and it is the log-normal case (attack A13). This result does not
depend on the tail question at all.

**The tails.** The evidence is mixed and weaker than it first appeared. The
working group reported seeing two to eight major event days a year where the
log-normal model predicts 2.27, which points to heavier tails. A Laplace model of
daily log-SAIDI predicts **5.32**, inside that range, and under it
the comparison fails at every window (A12). But the only formal fit test found,
on 278 Italian districts (Fumagalli et al. 2006), rejected log-normality in the
other direction, with tails thinner than normal. And US event-level tail indices
of 0.73 to 1.47 (Ahmad and Dobson 2025) turned out not to discriminate: this
repository's own tail test gives a wide log-normal a Hill index of 1.23, inside
that range. The tails remain unsettled. A13 is what settles part one.

**What the repository claims instead.** The thesis survives in a narrower form,
on filed data that involves no distributional assumption. Measurement choices
that never appear next to a published reliability figure change who looks
reliable and what the national number is. Which of the two published series a
benchmark ranks on moves half the large-utility field across quartiles (Q3), and
part two below shows that survives comparing utilities within like storm
exposure. How the national figure is averaged moves it by a factor of 2.07 (Q8),
and attack A9 shows that survives removing the ten largest contributors. The
threshold itself still matters: on the widest evidence window it moves fleet
SAIDI **1.52x** under Laplace tails and **3.16x** under
log-normal ones, roughly 50 to 120 minutes a year. That is material. It is not
more than utilities genuinely differ.

**Falsification criterion for the revised claim.** It fails if rank reversal
disappears within like storm exposure, or if the aggregation spread turns out to
be driven by a few utilities in the tail. Both have been run: part two below, and
attack A9. Both hold.

*Part two.* Rank reversal does not come from storm-exposed utilities trading
places with sheltered ones. Splitting the field by exposure measured from
2013-2018 filings and applied to 2022, so the stratifier is not the outcome,
quartile change runs 45%, 54% and 69% across exposure terciles against 52%
pooled, and 51% within non-hurricane states alone. It is if anything larger among
the most exposed.

One caveat on reading part two: median rank move falls from 18 pooled to 5-7
within strata, but that is mechanical, since a smaller field has fewer ranks to
move through. Quartile change is the statistic that compares across strata.

**Two kinds of answer.** Each question below is labelled by basis. *Filed data*
answers are computed directly from what utilities reported and depend on no
distributional assumption. *Model-implied* answers pass through the exclusion
identity in `docs/derivation.md` and inherit IEEE 1366's log-normal assumption;
the central model-implied comparison did not survive the evidence on realistic
threshold windows (A13) and is withdrawn above. Weight them accordingly.

**Sample.** 6,245 utility-state-years from EIA-861 reliability filings for 2013
through 2018 and 2022. Six consecutive years plus one later observation. The
2019-2021 filings are not public through any reachable mirror; see
`docs/DATA_ACCESS.md`.

**The setting.** IEEE 1366 lets a utility remove "major event days" from
reported SAIDI. A day qualifies if its SAIDI exceeds a threshold set 2.5
standard deviations up a log-normal fit to that utility's own five-year daily
outage history. The 2.5 was chosen by working-group consensus as a frequency
target, about 2.27 qualifying days a year, and compared against alternatives on
donated utility data; it was not derived from any reliability outcome. This
repository asks what that choice, and the surrounding discretion, is worth.

## Q1. How much of a reported SAIDI is the threshold parameter?

*Basis: model-implied.*

**Materially, though less than the first version said. Under IEEE 1366's own
log-normal model, sweeping the threshold from 1.5 to 3.5 moves customer-weighted
fleet SAIDI over a 7.66x range. On the thresholds the evidence supports, one to
fifteen qualifying days a year, the same records move it
3.16x under log-normal tails and 1.52x under Laplace ones.**

| threshold beta | fleet SAIDI without MED | vs the standard | expected qualifying days/yr |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5 | 37 min/yr | -68% | 24.4 |
| 2.0 | 67 min/yr | -43% | 8.3 |
| **2.5 (IEEE 1366)** | **115 min/yr** | baseline | **2.27 qualifying days** |
| 3.0 | 287 min/yr | +150% | 0.5 |

The standard's value also sits close to a cliff. Under log-normality the median
utility-year contains no qualifying day at all once beta passes about 2.95,
which is 0.45 above the published value.

**On the evidence window.** The table above is the log-normal model over a
sweep wider than any alternative the literature supports. The working group
designed 2.5 as a frequency, and the realistic range of frequencies runs from
one qualifying day a year to fifteen (LITERATURE.md). Across that window:

| daily log-SAIDI model | fleet SAIDI at 15 days/yr | at 1 day/yr | range |
|---|---|---|---|
| log-normal (the standard's) | 56.4 | 178.5 | 3.16x |
| Student-t, 15 df | 72.3 | 156.8 | 2.17x |
| Laplace | 95.2 | 145.2 | 1.52x |

Heavier tails shrink the effect, because a heavy-tailed utility already sends
more days past any threshold. The threshold choice is worth roughly 50 to 120
minutes a year of national SAIDI on realistic windows, which is the number to
quote.

**Prior work.** That the 2.5-beta threshold is unstable, and that alternative
definitions help some utilities while hurting others, is established (IEEE 2013;
LBNL, *Evaluating the Performance of the IEEE Standard 1366 Method*). The
contribution here is the magnitude at national scale and the route to measuring
it without daily data.

**How this is identified.** EIA-861 publishes annual aggregates, never daily
SAIDI, so no threshold can be recomputed directly. But the exclusion has a
closed form. If daily SAIDI is log-normal (the distribution IEEE 1366 itself
assumes), the share of annual outage minutes surviving exclusion is
`r = Phi(beta - sigma)`, which depends on the dispersion of daily SAIDI and on
nothing else: not the level, not utility size. Two published numbers therefore
pin the one latent parameter, and the sweep runs on that. Derivation in
`docs/derivation.md`. The closed form is a ratio of expectations and drifts
badly at high dispersion, so the tracks invert a simulated median-realization
surface instead, validated in Q7.

**What would overturn it.** The log-normal assumption, and this is not a
footnote. Attack A1 re-runs the whole inversion with heavier-tailed daily
outages, matched so every distribution reproduces the same observed exclusion
share. The direction survives everywhere (a lower threshold always excludes
more), but the sweep ratio spans **3.03x** across distributions and the cliff
disappears entirely. **The direction is a property of the rule. The magnitude is
a property of the log-normal.** Daily SAIDI series from any utility would settle
it.

## Q2. Do utilities cluster anywhere the rule would reward?

*Basis: filed data. The 67.2% admissibility figure is model-implied; the 29.8% mass point is not, and the benchmark it is compared against is the standard's own design frequency.*

**There is one large mass point, and it is not where a gaming story would put
it. 29.8% of filings report no major event days at all.**

**Prior work.** LBNL's survey of 123 utilities across 37 state commissions found
IEEE 1366 formally adopted by only four, a different major-event definition
required by fifteen, and no formal requirement at all in sixteen more. That
utilities are not applying a common rule is documented; what follows quantifies
it in the national filing panel.

Under the standard's own model this should be rare. A perfectly uniform utility,
at zero daily dispersion, still excludes 0.6% of its minutes, because a
continuous distribution always puts some mass above a finite threshold. Only
**67.2%** of filings fall inside what the model can produce.

A Chetty-style excess-mass estimator (fifth-degree counterfactual fitted
outside the bunching window, bootstrapped over utility-state clusters) puts the
mass at **12.0x** the counterfactual density (95% CI 7.6 to 26.2). It survives
33 of 36 alternative specifications; the three failures all widen the window to
0.10 with a flexible polynomial that can bend into it.

**Read the sign carefully.** Reporting zero exclusion makes a utility's headline
number worse, not better. This is not evidence that anyone is working the rule to
their advantage.

**What an identical pair means is not in doubt.** EIA's own filing FAQ tells a
utility that removes no data to report the same value in both columns. So the
mass point is a reporting practice, and the open question is how much of it is
genuine years with no qualifying day. The standard's design answers that. A
utility applying the 2.5-beta method should see a year with no qualifying day
about **10.3%** of the time, and at most
**13.5%** at the low end of the two to eight qualifying days a year the
working group actually observed.

| share reporting zero exclusion | 100 to 10k customers | 10k to 100k | 100k+ |
|---|---|---|---|
| IEEE 1366 filers | 46.2% | 23.9% | 12.2% |
| self-defined "Other" standard | 47.8% | 40.6% | 23.3% |

Large IEEE filers sit inside the range genuine segmentation produces. The excess
is concentrated in mid-size IEEE filers, in small utilities, where daily SAIDI is
lumpy with many zero days and the benchmark applies least well, and in
self-defined-standard filers, who report zero exclusion 1.7 to
1.9 times as often as IEEE filers of the same size. So the earlier
reading, that a large minority of utilities do not apply the methodology at all,
holds for small and self-defined-standard filers and does not hold for large IEEE
ones. It is still a comparability problem: a national SAIDI average pools
utilities using different measurement rules.

The choice of rule is itself discretionary. Utilities filing under a self-defined
"Other" standard rather than IEEE 1366 report a median retained share of
**0.883** against **0.700**, and 16.7% of utility-states switched standard at
least once across the seven years.

## Q3. Does the exclusion change *who* looks reliable?

*Basis: filed data.*

**Yes, for about half the field. Among large investor-owned utilities, 40 to 57%
change quartile depending on which of the two published series you rank on.**

**Prior work.** LBNL observed that the national with-major-events and
without-major-events averages are composed of different sets of utilities. That
is the qualitative form of what follows; the measurement is this repository's.

Benchmarking is comparative, and EIA-861 publishes two SAIDI series that rank
utilities differently. Across all seven filing years, the rank correlation
between them runs **0.610** to **0.767**, and the median utility moves 11 to 19
places in a field of roughly 120.

The extremes are large. Florida Power & Light files 1,150.5 min/yr with major
event days and 48.1 without, moving it from 123rd of 126 to 7th: a **116-place**
swing. Tampa Electric moves 122nd to 15th.

**This is not an accusation and reading it as one gets it backwards.** Gulf-coast
utilities have real hurricane days and the exclusion exists to remove them. The
point is narrower and harder to argue with: the two series answer different
questions. Without-MED asks how good your everyday distribution system is;
with-MED asks how long your customers were actually out. Both are legitimate.
Publishing one and calling it "reliability" is a decision, not a measurement.

## Q4. Is the decade's deterioration weather, or is it classification?

*Basis: filed data.*

**Both, and the seven-year panel separates them. The year-to-year swings are
weather. The slow drift into using the exclusion at all is methodology.**

On a balanced panel of **568 utility-states** present in every filing year,
three distinct signals appear that the earlier four-year version could not tell
apart:

**The everyday series trends, cleanly.** SAIDI without major event days rises
**3.21 min/yr** across 2013-2018 (p = 0.006, R² = 0.879). Slow, steady
deterioration in everyday distribution performance.

**The excluded portion does not trend: it spikes.** Its linear slope is large
but not statistically distinguishable from noise (p = 0.129, R² = 0.476),
because the series is dominated by 2017, where the customer-weighted gap is over
three times its 2015 level and then falls back the following year.

**The spikes are where the storms were.** This is the closest thing to a weather
test available without a severity index, and it is a placebo design: a list of
states is fixed from the 2017 Atlantic hurricane tracks (Harvey, Irma, Maria,
Nate) and applied unchanged to every year transition, so 2017 has to
distinguish itself rather than be selected. It does. Between 2016 and 2017 the
customer-weighted excluded minutes rose **655.9** minutes inside those states
against **107.7** elsewhere, a gap of **548.2** minutes; the next largest gap in
any transition is 252.2, and the following year it reverses. Meanwhile the
everyday series in those same states moved **0.1** minutes. A classification
drift does not know where hurricanes make landfall.

**But methodology adoption is real and separable.** The share of utilities
reporting no exclusion at all falls **1.08** percentage points per year
(p = 0.026). Across the panel, 19.0% of utilities went from reporting zero
exclusion to reporting some, against 10.4% moving the other way. Switching from
not applying the rule to applying it is a measurement change, not weather.

**How much of the national rise sits in the excluded portion, and for whom.**
Taken together, **82.9%** of the 2013-to-2022 rise in customer-weighted reported
SAIDI sits in the excluded portion. That single number turned out to be the least
robust thing in this repository, and the leave-one-state-out attack (A7) is what
found it:

| specification | share from the excluded part |
|---|---|
| headline, customer-weighted | 82.9% |
| 2013-2018 consecutive window | 88.0% |
| unweighted | 81.4% |
| top and bottom 1% of utility-years trimmed | 59.5% |
| **Florida alone removed** | **50.9%** |
| all hurricane-track states removed | **40.5%** |

Removing one state moves it 32 points. Removing the storm-exposed group halves
it. Every other headline in this repository survives leave-one-state-out, moving
by under 8.4% whichever state is dropped.

**The diagnosis is precise, and it is the interesting part.** Dropping the
hurricane states takes the *customer-weighted* share from 0.829 to 0.405 but the
*unweighted* share only from 0.814 to 0.743. So this is not a case of the
pattern being absent outside the storm belt. It is a case of a few very large
storm-exposed utilities dominating a customer-weighted aggregate. Florida Power
& Light alone files a 0.96 exclusion share on millions of customers.

**What survives untouched is the trend.** On the same seven-year balanced panel,
the everyday series rises **3.08** min/yr with all states (p = 0.008, R² = 0.860)
and **2.72** min/yr with the hurricane states removed (p = 0.006, R² = 0.878).
Dropping a fifth of the panel barely moves it. Trends are robust to dropping a
group; customer-weighted shares of an aggregate are not, when a handful of
utilities dominate the numerator.

**So Q4 has two answers, and only one of them is national.**

*National, and robust in direction:* everyday distribution reliability is
deteriorating, and the exclusion hides it from the headline number. Q8 tests this
against the averaging rule as well as against dropping states: all six
aggregators give a positive slope, but only four of six reach p < 0.05 and the
magnitude spans 1.64 to 4.61 minutes per year. The direction is unanimous. The
rate is not pinned down as tightly as the customer-weighted figure alone
suggests.

*Not national:* the claim that most of the reported deterioration sits in the
excluded portion is true for the customer-weighted US fleet, and true mainly
because of Florida and Texas. Outside the storm belt it is closer to a coin flip.
"US reliability is getting worse because of major events" is, on this data,
substantially a statement about the Gulf coast.

That is itself worth reporting rather than patching over. **A national SAIDI
average is not a national quantity.** It is a customer-weighted mean over a
population whose tail is a few enormous hurricane-exposed utilities, and any
benchmark built on it inherits that.

## Q5. Does capital deployment show up in reported reliability?

*Basis: filed data.*

**No, and this version of the answer is worth something because the design could
have seen it.**

There is no spending variable anywhere in this repository (FERC Form 1 is
unreachable), so the regressor is physical deployment: AMI share of customers.

An earlier version ran a 2022 cross-section, found nothing and called it a null.
That was an artefact of timing: by 2022 the interquartile range of AMI share is
0.97 to 1.00 and 55% of utility-states sit at exactly 1.00. A regressor with no
spread cannot detect anything.

The 2016-2018 filings fix it. In 2016 the interquartile range is 0.04 to 1.00.
589 utility-states are observed in all three years and **53** move their AMI
share by more than 0.5 inside that window: a rollout observed within the same
utility.

| design | coefficient on AMI share | 95% CI | detectable at 80% power |
|---|---|---|---|
| cross-section 2016 | -0.129 | -0.283 to +0.024 | 22% SAIDI change |
| cross-section 2022 | -0.176 | -0.402 to +0.051 | 28% |
| **within-utility, 2016-2018** | **+0.036** | **-0.159 to +0.231** | **24%** |
| long difference, 2016-2018 | +0.049 | -0.197 to +0.295 | 30% |

The blunt version agrees: the 49 utilities that rolled AMI out over the window
changed their everyday SAIDI by **-3.0%** relative to the 464 that did not move,
which is economically small and statistically nowhere.

**What this does and does not say.** It says that within a utility, converting
the meter fleet to AMI is not followed by a detectable change in reported
everyday reliability, in a design that could have caught a 24% effect. It does
not say distribution capital has no effect: AMI is a metering technology whose
reliability benefit runs through outage detection and restoration speed, and no
spending variable was available to test anything broader.

A variance decomposition caps what any utility-level regressor could reach:
across 2013-2018, **59%** of the variance in log SAIDI is a persistent utility
effect and 41% is year-to-year shock.

## Q6. Does ownership structure predict exclusion?

*Basis: filed data.*

**Partly, and one half of this finding was retracted when the panel doubled.**

> **Retracted.** The four-year version of this repository reported that investor
> ownership lowers the probability of reporting zero exclusion by 7.5 percentage
> points once size is controlled (p = 0.026), and treated that as the surviving
> ownership effect. On the seven-year panel the same specification gives 3.6
> points at p = 0.199. It is not distinguishable from zero and should not have
> been reported as a finding.

Raw medians still order as expected: retained share is **0.643** for
investor-owned, 0.740 cooperative, **0.901** municipal.

What survives conditioning is the intensive margin, not the extensive one. With
log customers, state and year controlled, investor ownership is associated with a
retained share **0.045** lower than cooperatives (p = 0.023): IOUs exclude
*more* when they exclude. Whether a utility excludes at all is explained by size,
not ownership.

**And this cannot separate the two obvious stories.** IOUs are roughly fifteen
times larger than co-ops by customer count. A regulatory-scrutiny explanation and
a "large utilities have outage management systems capable of classifying days"
explanation both predict exactly this pattern. Attack A5 shrinks the raw gap from
0.097 to 0.027 by restricting to above-median-size utilities.

## Q7. Is the source data internally consistent?

*Basis: filed data.*

**Yes, to eight significant figures at the 99th percentile of filings.**

CAIDI is definitionally SAIDI / SAIFI, and EIA-861 collects all three
independently in the 2016-2018 and 2022 files. The identity reproduces within 1%
in **100.00%** of 3,838 filings. Whatever else is wrong with these numbers, they
are not arithmetic errors.

The ingest layer also surfaces what it discards: 817 rows fail the analysis
filter and are reported by reason, and 12 rows are genuine duplicate
utility-state-year filings in the 2014 source, kept at the larger customer count.

## Q8. How much of the *national* number is the averaging rule?

*Basis: filed data for the 2.07x spread; the 14.32x combined space is model-implied.*

**Enough that the aggregation choice rivals the threshold choice. Averaging the
same 2022 filings five defensible ways gives national SAIDI anywhere from 173.7
to 359.5 min/yr with major event days (a 2.07x range) before anyone touches
the exclusion.**

**Prior work.** The comparability problem is recognised and has a convention:
APPA's benchmarking reports state that utility statistics are customer-weighted
when aggregated. What was not found is a measurement of how far the national
figure moves across defensible alternatives.

"US reliability" is not a utility number, it is an aggregate, and aggregating
needs a second choice nobody writes down. Attack A7 forced the question by
showing the customer-weighted figure is dominated by a few enormous storm-exposed
utilities.

| 2022 aggregator | with MED | without MED | the question it answers |
|---|---|---|---|
| unweighted mean | 359.5 | 146.3 | the average utility |
| customer-weighted mean | 338.7 | 125.7 | total burden per customer |
| winsorized cw mean | 319.1 | 125.4 | burden with catastrophe capped |
| trimmed cw mean | 311.1 | 124.6 | burden with catastrophe dropped |
| customer-weighted median | 215.6 | 101.4 | what the median *customer* saw |
| median utility | 173.7 | 103.0 | the typical utility |

All six are defensible and they answer different questions. The customer-weighted
mean, the one in general use, is the most exposed to the tail, and the tail is
extreme. One utility-state supplies **13.1%** of national customer-outage-minutes
with major event days included; the top ten supply **38.6%**; the Gini across
filings is **0.863**.

**The two choices compound.** A national SAIDI figure requires picking a
threshold *and* picking an aggregator, and in most places the number appears
neither is stated. Sweeping both over defensible values gives 2022 national SAIDI
anywhere from **27.2** to **388.8** min/yr: a **14.32x** range. The conventional
pair of choices lands at 129.7.

**What this does to Q4.** It scopes it, in the direction of honesty. Q4's
everyday deterioration trend was robust to dropping any state. Tested against the
averaging rule it is robust in *direction* (all six aggregators give a positive
slope), but only four of six reach p < 0.05, and the slope ranges from 1.64 to
4.61 min/yr. The two that miss significance are the unweighted mean and the
customer-weighted median, which are the two most resistant to the tail. That is
consistent with the deterioration being real but concentrated rather than uniform.

**The practical implication is narrow and checkable.** Any published national
reliability figure should state its averaging rule alongside its exclusion rule.
Neither is a detail, and together they span more than an order of magnitude.

## Two smaller results

**Cause-specific exclusions stack.** 88.4% of IEEE-standard filers also report a
loss-of-supply variant, removing outages originating upstream of distribution.
It removes a median of 3.6% of outage minutes (retained share **0.964**), 7.3%
customer-weighted. Stacked with the major-event-day exclusion, the median
utility-year reports 58.8% of its raw outage minutes.

**Undergrounding misses breakeven by an order of magnitude at average density.**
A payback model, not a measurement, with parameters sourced inline. At the
national average of **30.5 customers per** line mile, $3.8M per circuit mile,
$370.2 per customer-hour, 30-year life at 7%, and the *most favourable* published
reliability benefit (99.6%), the baseline SAIDI needed to break even is
**1,633 min/yr**. The median 2022 utility reports 173.7. Substituting the
econometric estimate for the case-study one moves breakeven by 7.3x: the two
literatures disagree by more than any parameter in the model. Density decides it:
at 400 customers per mile breakeven falls to 125 min/yr, below the national
median, which is consistent with undergrounding being justified in dense urban
territory and on wildfire grounds rather than on avoided outage minutes.

## The thread

Every question lands in the same place from a different direction: **what gets
reported is decided more by measurement rules than by anything on the poles.**

The threshold parameter moves fleet SAIDI 7.66x. Half the large-IOU field
changes quartile depending on which published series you rank on. Nearly a third
of utilities do not apply the exclusion rule at all, and which standard they file
under moves their number more than ownership does. Two carve-outs stack to remove
41% of median outage minutes. And the one thing that should move the number
(capital deployment) cannot be detected inside a utility even when the rollout is
observed directly.

And the aggregation rule adds a second order of magnitude on top of the first:
threshold and averaging together span 14.32x for a single year.

The everyday series is the exception that makes the point. Strip the exclusions away
and what remains is a slow, boring, highly significant 3.21 minutes per year of
deterioration, with an R² of 0.879. That is probably the real signal, and it is
the one nobody reports.

For a regulator the implication is specific: specify the measurement rule as
tightly as the target, and state which series a benchmark is built on.

## What would change these conclusions

- **Daily SAIDI from any utility** would replace Q1's model-implied sweep with a
  measurement and settle attack A1 outright.
- **FERC Form 1** would give Q5 a spending variable and unblock the
  vegetation-management question entirely.
- **A storm severity index keyed to service territories** would turn Q4's
  geographic placebo into a direct test.
- **The 2019-2021 filings** would close the remaining gap and let Q4 date the
  post-2018 movement rather than inferring it from endpoints.
